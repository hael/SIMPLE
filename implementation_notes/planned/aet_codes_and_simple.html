    How the atomic electron tomography codes relate to SIMPLE and SINGLE¶

    Reference note, 2026-10-14. It compares the codes of Jianwei Miao's group (UCLA) with what SIMPLE and SINGLE do today and plan to do. The reconstruction gets the most attention: their GENFIRE algorithm against our PCG backend.

    Sources¶

    What we have read. Peter's folder (~/Downloads/TomoFromPeter) holds the full MATLAB code of the 2017 FePt study (Yang et al., Nature 542, 75):

    • GENFIRE v1 and its angle refinement;
    • atom tracing and species classification;
    • the final position refinement;
    • the data: the tilt series, the reconstruction, the traced peaks and the models.

    On our side the sources are:

    • doc/algorithms/reconstruction.md and doc/policies/3D/reconstruct3D_pcg_policy.md;
    • pcg_backend_overview.md;
    • single_pcg_integration.md;
    • species_discovery.md;
    • atom_bfactor_model.md;
    • the code those documents point to.

    Read later. The code of the 2021 metallic-glass study (Yang et al., Nature 592, 60), cloned from AET-MetallicGlass/Supplementary-Data-Codes into ~/src/Supplementary-Data-Codes. It contains:

    • RESIRE, their current reconstruction algorithm;
    • polynomial atom tracing;
    • k-means classification with local templates;
    • position refinement.

    What we have not read. The later repository AET-pAET/Supplementary-Data-Codes. Where this note describes work after 2021, it relies on the published method descriptions, not on code.

    Terms.

    • AET (atomic electron tomography). One nanoparticle is imaged at many tilts in a scanning transmission electron microscope, here with an annular dark-field detector (ADF-STEM). The tilt series is reconstructed to a 3D map in which single atoms are resolved.
    • SINGLE. Our method for nanoparticles in a graphene liquid cell. It uses thousands of transmission electron microscope images of a freely rotating particle, each of them very noisy.
    • Gridding. Each observed Fourier sample is spread onto the regular 3D Fourier grid with interpolation weights. The map is the weighted sum divided by the weights.
    • PCG. Preconditioned conjugate gradients, our iterative least-squares reconstruction.

    1. Two problems that end in the same place¶

    AET (Miao group) SINGLE
    Sample one particle, in vacuum, on a support one particle, in liquid, rotating freely
    Images a few tens of projections of the same particle (68 for FePt), high dose each hundreds to a few thousand particle images, low dose each
    Orientations one tilt axis, about ±65°; a missing wedge of directions random; usually no missing directions
    Image formation ADF-STEM: incoherent, intensity rises steeply with atomic number, no transfer function phase contrast: contrast transfer function (CTF), weaker dependence on atomic number
    Orientation estimate known tilts, refined by a small grid search unknown, estimated per image in every refinement round
    Pre-processing alignment and denoising of each projection (BM3D in their recent code) motion correction, CTF estimation, 2D and 3D refinement
    Map 256³ at 0.3725 Å (FePt) boxes of 120 to 200 at about 0.36 Å

    The end products are the same. Both produce a 3D map in which atoms are resolved, an atomic model traced from it (positions, then species), and statistics on that model: strain, coordination, chemical order.

    The hard parts differ. AET has plenty of signal per image but missing directions, so its reconstruction must fill in data that were never measured. SINGLE has all directions but very little signal per image, and unknown orientations. Its reconstruction must average noise correctly across thousands of images, inside an alternation with the orientation search.

    2. The pipelines side by side¶

    Stage AET (FePt 2017 code, and later papers) SIMPLE / SINGLE today Planned on our side

    3. Reconstruction: GENFIRE and PCG¶

    3.1 What GENFIRE does¶

    This is read from GENFIRE_main_FePt_FinalReconstruction.m, My_fill_cubicgrid_ver1.m and GENFIRE_iterate.m.

    1. Gridding.
    2. Each projection is zero-padded four times (oversamplingRatio = 4), so the Fourier grid is four times finer than the image grid.
    3. For every grid point within 0.05 grid units of a projection's central plane, the exact discrete Fourier transform of that projection is evaluated at the foot of the perpendicular.
    4. Where several projections reach the same grid point, their values are averaged with inverse-distance weights.
    5. Every other grid point is marked as unmeasured. So the data enter as nearly exact Fourier values on a sparse set of grid points. There is no interpolation kernel and no noise weight.
    6. Iteration. It starts from the inverse transform of the gridded data and repeats 500 times:
    7. set negative values to zero (positivity);
    8. set values outside the support to zero;
    9. Fourier transform;
    10. overwrite every measured grid point with its measured value; leave the unmeasured ones as they are;
    11. inverse transform.

    The iterate with the smallest Fourier error is kept. That error is the L1 distance between the measured and computed amplitudes at the measured points. 3. Options used for FePt. Measured values are enforced out to 1.5 times the Nyquist radius, which takes in most of the cube's corners. The support is a smoothed cube. An R-free test exists: it withholds 5% of the measured points in the outer shell to find the resolution the data support. The final FePt run does not use it. 4. Angle refinement. One Euler angle is refined at a time, over a grid of ±2.1° in 0.3° steps. Each candidate is scored by the L1 R-factor between a measured projection and the reprojection of a GENFIRE reconstruction, after a centre-of-mass shift.

    3.2 What PCG does¶

    This is the short version; the policy is the contract.

    • The least-squares problem. PCG solves the weighted least-squares problem of the projection-slice forward model, (P H P + lambda I) u = P b:
    • H = sum_i K_i^T K_i, where K_i gathers a central section with the Kaiser-Bessel kernel on a 2× oversampled grid and applies the shift, the CTF and the noise weight 1/sigma2;
    • b is the weighted backprojection;
    • P is a soft or hard real-space support;
    • lambda is a small ridge.
    • The operator. H is applied as one convolution on the padded lattice (the Toeplitz kernel), so an iteration costs a few FFTs whatever the number of particles.
    • The preconditioner. The Fourier-diagonal preconditioner is 1/(rho + floor). Here rho is the sampling density: the sum, over images, of the CTF squared over the noise power deposited at each grid point.
    • Iterations. The solve starts from zero and runs two iterations in refinement, five for final maps.
    • Regularization. Under ML regularization, a second, regularized map is the closed-form Fourier-shell shrinkage of the first, using the FSC-derived signal prior. SINGLE turns ML regularization off.
    • No positivity. The solver is linear.

    3.3 How the two relate¶

    Written as formulas, GENFIRE is a simplified version of the problem PCG solves, plus one constraint PCG cannot carry.

    Let M be the indicator of the measured grid points, m the gridded values, F the Fourier transform and C the projection onto non-negative maps inside the support. One GENFIRE iteration is

    x_{n+1} = z - F^{-1} M (F z - m),        z = C(x_n).

    This is one gradient step, of size one, on the gridded data misfit 1/2 ||M (F z - m)||^2, taken from the point z that satisfies the constraints. In other words, GENFIRE is projected gradient descent. Error reduction in phase retrieval has long been read the same way.

    Now take PCG with the gridding approximation of its operator. Without the Kaiser-Bessel coupling, H becomes the Fourier-diagonal density rho, and b becomes the gridding backprojection B. One preconditioned gradient step of unit size is then

    u_{n+1} = u_n - F^{-1} [rho/(rho + floor)] (F P u_n - B/rho).

    Where the data are dense (rho >> floor), the factor in brackets is 1 and B/rho is the gridded measurement. Where nothing was measured, it is 0. That is GENFIRE's update with M replaced by a smooth version of itself and without positivity. Both methods also start from the same point: the first iterate of either is the gridding reconstruction inside the support.

    PCG improves on that iteration in four ways:

    • conjugate directions with optimal step lengths instead of a fixed unit step, so the linear part converges in far fewer iterations;
    • the exact coupled operator (Kaiser-Bessel interpolation, about 3% kernel error) instead of the diagonal gridding approximation;
    • data weights |CTF|^2/sigma2, so that thousands of noisy observations of one grid point are averaged by their reliability rather than overwritten by one value;
    • a statistical regularizer (the FSC prior).

    GENFIRE has one thing PCG lacks: positivity, a nonlinear constraint that conjugate gradients cannot carry. With positivity and a tight support, the iteration fills Fourier regions that no projection sampled, such as the missing wedge, the gaps between few projections, and the corners of the cube. Without positivity a support still couples measured and unmeasured Fourier points. On the FePt tilt series PCG at first filled them harmfully, through a preconditioner defect that is now fixed (3.5).

    3.4 What the differences mean for SINGLE¶

    • Missing data against noisy data. GENFIRE's main strength is recovering what was never measured. SINGLE usually measures every direction, many times, with very little signal each time. For SINGLE the noise weighting, the CTF and the averaging across images matter more, and GENFIRE has none of the three. Run on SINGLE data, GENFIRE would replace each grid point by the average of whatever samples landed within 0.05 units, with no CTF correction.
    • Positivity as a prior for SINGLE. The atomic potential above the liquid background is positive, so positivity is physically valid. At low signal-to-noise, though, clipping negative noise biases the map upward, most in the solvent and at weak atoms, which are exactly where species discovery looks. If we want it, it needs a projected or bound-constrained solver, not plain CG. On FePt it does not pay for atom detection (3.5): it makes the atoms rounder and the map free of negative values, but it does not lift more atoms above the background, and recall fell. The atomic prior of single_pcg_integration.md (penalize what is not a sum of atom kernels) is a stronger, linear alternative that keeps CG. It also enforces vacuum between atoms, which is the part of positivity that matters for atom detection.
    • Iterations and regularization. GENFIRE runs 500 iterations and keeps the best one by Fourier error. That error is measured on the same data it enforces, so it cannot detect over-fitting; the R-free option can. PCG stops early on purpose: two iterations are the calibrated regime, and over-iteration breaks the Toeplitz approximation. Both methods rely on stopping as part of their regularization.
    • Cost. GENFIRE's exact gridding costs about the number of projections, times the grid points near each plane, times the pixels per projection. That is affordable for 68 projections and impossible for thousands of images. PCG reads every image once, and its iterations do not depend on the number of images.
    • RESIRE. Their current algorithm (Pham et al., Sci. Rep. 2023), read from 2_RESIRE_package/src/@RESIRE_Reconstructor/reconstruct.m. It minimizes the squared difference between the measured and computed projections by gradient descent with a fixed step:
    • Computing projections. The volume is padded four times and Fourier transformed. The central plane of each projection is interpolated with splines, then transformed back to real space.
    • The gradient. It is the real-space back-projection of the computed projections, minus the back-projection of the measured ones (computed once, before the loop).
    • The step. The step is fixed, scaled by the number of projections times the box size. Negative values are set to zero after every step. The default run is 200 iterations.
    • What is not there. The loop has no support constraint (a support can only serve as the starting model) and no noise weights. The forward and back projections use different interpolations, so they are not exact adjoints.

    This is PCG's objective without the weights, solved by fixed-step gradient descent with positivity instead of conjugate gradients. It is also GENFIRE with the misfit measured on the projections rather than on gridded Fourier points. Their field has moved toward our formulation, but it keeps positivity and has no noise model.

    3.5 A direct comparison we can run¶

    SIMPLE can reconstruct a tilt series. The projections become particles with known orientations (after converting their Euler convention), ctf=no and the correlation objective. The FePt data allow a like-for-like comparison: reconstruct3D rec_backend=pcg on their projections against their GENFIRE volume, which is in the folder.

    It needs the denoised projections and refined angles from the group's FePt page; the folder holds only the raw 300² projections. The comparison would show three things:

    • how much of their map's quality comes from positivity in the missing wedge;
    • what PCG's support does there without positivity;
    • whether our atom tracing and species calls change between the two maps.

    First run, 2026-10-15. It used the denoised projections with the initial single-axis angles (for_simple/run_fept_recon_only.sh), and the frame and hand came out right.

    • Gridding works. The half-map FSC reaches 0.5 at 3.4 Å.
    • PCG did not. It reached 0.5 at 6.6 Å after one iteration and 9.2 Å after two, with a checkerboard spike at the map centre, 13% of its power beyond the Nyquist sphere and a halo in the missing wedge. One defect caused all three: the right-hand side is cut by the solve support before the Fourier divide, and in voxels no projection measured the preconditioner was only the floor, about a hundredfold gain. The cause, the fix and its test are in doc/refactoring_notes/completed/pcg_sparse_sampling_fix.md.

    After the fix, same day. PCG reaches 0.5 at 3.41 Å after two iterations and 3.31 Å after five, and correlates better than gridding with a numpy filtered back-projection (0.919 and 0.911 against 0.896). Level-1 atom detection, against their final model, finds:

    Map Recall Precision
    SIMPLE gridding 0.25 0.99
    SIMPLE PCG, 2 iterations 0.26 0.99
    SIMPLE PCG, 5 iterations 0.35 0.99
    Their GENFIRE map 0.41 1.00

    With species discovery on the half maps, PCG at five iterations finds 54% of the atoms at 98% precision (gridding 41%). The species calls are wrong: the detected atoms are almost all Pt, since few Fe atoms rise above the background in any of the maps, and discovery splits the Pt into two groups.

    Positivity. Their atoms look rounder, which raised the question whether positivity is the reason. A numpy GENFIRE-style iteration on our data and nominal angles (measured Fourier points put back each iteration, a sphere as support), with and without clipping negative values:

    • It rounds the atoms. The mean Pt atom is 2.02 times longer along the missing-wedge direction than across without positivity, 1.90 after 200 iterations with it. Their map: 1.89.
    • It does not separate atoms from the background. The share of Pt atoms brighter than the 99th percentile of the background between atoms stays at 85% (theirs 100%).
    • It lowers detection. Level-1 recall is 0.30 without positivity and 0.24 with it.

    So positivity explains the look of their map, not its better detection.

    Refined angles. refine3D refine=cont refines each projection's three rotation components and two shifts against the PCG map of the nominal tilts (for_simple/run_fept_refine_cont.sh). It converged in two iterations and moved the projections by 0.31 degrees on average (largest 1.1 degrees) and 0.2 pixels, with no rotation of the whole frame. PCG at five iterations from the refined poses raises level-1 recall from 0.35 to 0.39, at precision 0.99 (their map: 0.41), and recall with discovery from 0.54 to 0.62. The share of Pt atoms above the background rises from 66% to 71% (theirs 90%, smoothed measure). Angle refinement in our own pipeline therefore closes most of the detection gap to their map. The SINGLE workflow refine_tomo packages these steps, with a density mask as the PCG support and, by default, two iterations at 2 A: longer refinement stretches the tilt range and rotates the frame, which the data barely constrain; import_tilt_series reads their angle convention and scripts/aet_mat2simple.py their MATLAB files (doc/refactoring_notes/planned/refine_tomo.md).

    4. Atom tracing¶

    AET detect_atoms
    Candidate atoms every local maximum above a threshold, brightest first voxels of the template-correlation map above a threshold, chosen by how well the resulting atoms simulate the map, grouped into connected components
    Separating close atoms each local maximum is its own atom; a 2 Å minimum distance (in 2017 the fitted Gaussian of each accepted atom was also subtracted before the next maximum was taken) merged components are split, validated against the correlation map
    Position 2017: centre of an anisotropic 3D Gaussian fitted in a 5 × 5 × 5 box; 2021: maximum of a 4th-order polynomial fitted on the 3× upsampled map centre of mass of the component, after the splitting
    False atoms a "non-atom" class in the classification, then manual removal correlation validation and contact-score pruning (weak, poorly coordinated atoms are removed on purpose)
    Weak atoms found by the same greedy loop as strong ones level 1 finds the strong class; discover_species recovers weaker atoms from the residual at calibrated noise thresholds, with a neighbour gate

    Their tracing needs no segmentation and treats every atom alike. Ours segments first. The residual recovery of discover_species is their 2017 idea applied only below the strong class, with thresholds set against a measured noise level instead of a fixed intensity threshold and a manual check. In the papers we looked at, hand edits are part of their pipeline. Ours has none, and that is a design constraint we keep.

    The FePt map shows what segmenting first costs on a dense two-species particle (2026-10-15). With element=Pt, level 1 found 57% of the Pt atoms and none of the Fe. Every atom it found is real, within 0.14 Å of their refined position.

    The atoms it missed are not merged into components with found ones. They lie below the threshold. The search chose that threshold because lower ones merge neighbouring atoms: the correlation between the map and the simulated atoms falls from 0.50 to 0.28 as the threshold drops. A tracer built on local maxima does not tie the threshold to merging.

    5. Species¶

    Their methods across papers:

    • FePt (2017). A threshold on the 3 × 3 × 3-voxel intensity; global 5 × 5 × 5 templates matched by L1 distance and iterated; a test against zero for non-atoms; manual edits; reclassification of Fe atoms near the missing wedge with a separate threshold.
    • The high-entropy metallic glass (2021) and nucleation (2025) studies. K-means on integrated intensity into three types and non-atoms, then local templates: each atom goes to the type whose mean within 10 Å it is closest to.
    • The PtNi catalysts (KAIST group, 2025). K-means into non-atom, Ni and Pt, with an Otsu boundary.

    None of these models how an atom's environment changes its intensity. On the FePt volume (species_discovery_status.md):

    • intensity rises by 5.2% per neighbour for both species;
    • the classes are separated by D = 2.6;
    • the share of Fe at the surface is 65 to 68% in their model, 86% from a plain mixture fit and 20% from the same fit with our coordination term.

    Local templates do not remove the trend (87% at the surface), because the 10 Å neighbourhood of a surface atom is mostly bulk.

    Ours: a mixture with an explicit measurement noise, a criterion for the number of classes, a separation gate, and one shared coordination slope. The comparison says the environment model is the decisive choice for surface species. Neither group can settle it from map intensity alone.

    6. Refining the model against the data¶

    Their final refinement (XYZ_Refinement_Main.m):

    • The computed data. Each atom contributes, in Fourier space, a common form factor (the average of the Kirkland Fe and Pt factors) times a per-species amplitude and a per-species Debye-Waller term. The amplitudes are 0.56 and 1.18; the B factors are 21.9 and 20.1 Å² in the usual convention. The sum is compared with the transforms of the 68 measured projections, inside a resolution sphere, after a least-squares scale.
    • The steps. Positions move along the gradient of the squared difference plus a weighted gradient of an embedded-atom potential for FePt. Each step has a fixed total length. After 150 steps, the configuration with the smallest data error is kept.
    • What stays fixed. Species and B are not refined per atom, and there is no occupancy.

    On our side:

    • Today. Positions come from the map, and _SIM.mrc (Peng's five-Gaussian electron scattering factors per element, with a B) only seeds the next refinement round.
    • The atomic prior (single_pcg_integration.md). It puts the model inside the reconstruction: lambda_A (I - P_G) penalizes density outside the span of the atom kernels, with amplitudes free and scale never compared. Its strong-prior limit is their refinement's model (a sum of atoms fitted to the data), reached through the image data rather than through a separate refinement.
    • The B-factor note's preferred route. It fits a smooth per-atom B and occupancy profile against the particle images. That is their refinement's objective with the two per-atom quantities they lack.

    Their B factors are of the same size as the total B we measured in the core of real SINGLE Pt maps (about 21 Å²).

    A related line of work, local-orbital tomography (Rong Yu's group, Tsinghua), makes atoms the unknowns of the reconstruction, with a depth-dependent probe. That is the far end of the atomic-prior ladder.

    7. Validation practice¶

    • Their checks. They simulate their own final model with a multislice code, run the simulated projections through the same pipeline, and report the fraction of atoms recovered with the right position and species. They also report the R-factor of the model against the measured projections. These checks test self-consistency, not truth.
    • The independent critique. Busch et al. (Nature, 2026) argue, from simulations, that species identification becomes very uncertain once atom-peak intensities overlap the background. The group's reply in the same issue reports better recovery, but also on simulated data, so the question stays open for real data.
    • Ours. Ground truth exists only for synthetic particles (element kernels, realistic noise). On real maps we test single-species maps for false species and plant weaker classes. The FePt volume is now our first real two-species map, with their model as a reference, not as truth.

    8. What we take from them¶

    • The FePt data, as the first real two-species test of detect_atoms, with and without the coordination term (species_discovery_status.md). It is also a reconstruction test: PCG against GENFIRE on the same projections (3.5), once the denoised projections and angles are downloaded.
    • Positivity, considered as a prior for SINGLE. It has a known low-signal bias, and the planned atomic prior covers its useful part within a linear solve.
    • Refinement against the images, not the map, for positions and, unlike theirs, per-atom B and occupancy. It is the one place where occupancy and species might be told apart.
    • An R-free style hold-out for every iterative or fitted step. It is their GENFIRE option and our planned image-level cross-validation, in place of a fit criterion measured on the data being fitted.
    • What we keep: no manual edits, explicit noise models, and an environment term reported with every surface species call.
