    Polar kernels on x86: the report¶

    Report of Phases 3 and 4 of the plan polar_kernel_codegen.md (same directory), the x86 follow-up of the Mac run committed as ee5b68be1. Unattended run kernel_x86 on the Dell (Intel Core Ultra 9 285: 8 performance cores with their own L2, 16 efficiency cores in clusters of four sharing an L2; gfortran 15.2.1, -O3 -march=native, system OpenBLAS 0.3.15, FFTW 3.3.5), 2026-10-22/23. The only production file changed is src/main/pftc/simple_polarft_corr.f90. Nothing is committed.

    The answer¶

    • Why x86 was slower. With the sums in helpers with plain array arguments, gcc can prove that two shells may be processed in one pass and applies unroll-and-jam to the shell loop. On the Mac that gave two shells per pass with de-interleaving loads and stores, a gain. On x86 with AVX2 gcc vectorizes the jammed loop with lane permutes (36 vpermd per pass), reloads of permute constants and register spills: 17 instructions per bin and shell against 15 for the old loop. Permutes are slow on the efficiency cores most of all, hence HEAD's euclid kernel at 0.81x of the old one there and 0.92x on a performance core. The old loops could not be jammed, because their accumulator is a pointer component; they were already vectorized at full AVX2 width.
    • The form kept (c14). The euclid sums call a helper once per shell (euclid_shell_sum), the cc sums call one once per two shells (cc_shells_sum). The helpers still take plain array arguments, so the compiler knows unit stride and no overlap (what the Mac needed), but there is no shell loop around a bin loop in one procedure left to jam. Each element's expression and precision are unchanged; the distance conversion of ee5b68be1 stays.
    • Its speed against the old form (f3ccb5fbd) here: euclid 1.15x on a performance core, 1.13x on an efficiency core, 1.00-1.28x in teams of 12 and 24 threads; cc 1.39x and 1.31x alone, 1.22-1.49x in teams. Against HEAD the euclid kernel is 1.24x faster on a performance core and 1.39x on an efficiency core.
    • Identity. Distances differ from the old form's by at most one ulp, two in the shifted-reference branch and in the bank's exp(-loss) values; the best rotation of a pair never changes; 6 of 320,000 lists of the 20 lowest rotations change order (HEAD: 1 for euclid, 4 for cc). cc distances (1 - cc): at most one ulp, 99.9 % of the vectors byte-identical.
    • The Mac. Speed is judged on the Dell, which is closer to the machines SIMPLE normally runs on; the Mac only has to build and pass its checks (maintainer, 2026-10-23). Whether c14 keeps the Mac's euclid gain of ee5b68be1 (its euclid now runs one shell per pass there too) is not measured.

    What changed, phase by phase¶

    • Phase 3 (review/phase_3.diff under ~/agent_runs/kernel_x86): the plan gained Phases 3-4, the judging rule, a file table and the Phase 3 progress (diagnosis, every candidate's timings, identity). The benchmark driver simple_commanders_test_highlevel_matchbench.f90 times its middle team at half the threads (24 -> 24, 12, 1 on the Dell; 11 -> 11, 5, 1 on the Mac as before). No production change.
    • Phase 4 (review/phase_4.diff): simple_polarft_corr.f90 gets the c14 form: euclid_sums calls the new euclid_shell_sum once per shell; cc_sums calls the new cc_shells_sum once per two shells (once more for an odd last shell). Callers, argument passing and the distance conversion are HEAD's. This report and the plan's Progress complete the phase.

    Why HEAD was slower here¶

    Read from gfortran's vectorization and loop reports (-fopt-info-vec-all, -fopt-info-loop-optimized) and the assembly of the hot loops, with the build's flags:

    • The old euclid loops vectorize 4 complex values per iteration (32-byte vectors), behind a run-time overlap check. The accumulator crvec1(ithr)%c is a pointer component of unknown stride, so it is loaded and stored 8 bytes at a time: 61 instructions per 4 bins.
    • HEAD's euclid_sums contains a clean loop of 50 instructions per 4 bins, but it runs only for the odd last shell. For more than two shells the code enters the unroll-and-jammed version (gcc reports "applying unroll and jam with factor 2" for the shell loops of euclid_sums and both of cc_sums): 276 instructions per 8 bins x 2 shells, of them 36 vpermd (cross-lane permutes), 16 vblendps, 18 reloads of permute constants and 24 stack accesses for spilled registers. The cc loop has 20 vpermd per iteration; the old cc loop none.
    • No packing copies (_gfortran_internal_pack) in either form's hot path; the FFTW buffer is 32-byte aligned, so HEAD's vector stores never split a cache line; the distance conversion vectorizes without masked stores and is not involved (byte-identical; alone it makes the old sums 2.7 % faster on a performance core).
    • What does not help: !$omp simd on the bin loop and !GCC$ unroll 1 on the shell loop leave the jam in place; a local pointer, contiguous alias of the accumulator does not change gfortran's code at all (still strided).
    • Writing two shells per pass by hand removes the permutes and is the fastest form on one thread (c6, c12, c13: euclid 1.15x), but it reads eight memory streams per pass (four arrays, two shells) and falls to 0.88-0.96x at 12 threads. The likely reason, not proven: cores that share an L2 run out of hardware prefetch streams. Forms that read at most four streams per pass (one euclid shell, or two cc shells of two arrays) keep their gain in a team. This is why c14 treats the two objectives differently.

    The candidates in every setting¶

    How measured: each form is HEAD's file with only the sums and their callers replaced, built in one scratch tree; the benchmark's production layouts per_pair (euclid) and per_pair_cc on the cases 1000x1 (1,000 references) and 2500x4 (10,000). P1 and E1: one thread pinned to cpu3 (performance) and cpu20 (efficiency), best of three after a warm-up, median of two or three pinned rounds (repeatable within 1 %). T12 and T24: unpinned teams as production runs them; they move 10-30 % from launch to launch, so six rounds in rotating order (c14 four, HEAD two), median. Nothing else ran during the timings. Speed = old time / form time, 1000x1 / 2500x4; the old row gives µs per pair and thread; team cells give the number of rounds. Forms without team timings were already outside the rule on one thread (c4, c5), share their euclid loop with a timed form (c6 with c12 and c13), equal the reference (c1), or are diagnostics (d1); c7 is the old-loop version of the two-shell form.

    Commit message¶

    pftc: per-shell helper calls for the euclid and cc sums

With the shell loop and the bin loop in one helper, gcc unroll-and-jams two
shells; on x86 (AVX2) the jammed loop needs lane permutes and register spills,
and the euclid kernel ran at 0.81-0.92x of the old inline loops. The euclid
sums now call a helper once per shell and the cc sums once per two shells, still
with plain array arguments (unit stride, no overlap). On the Dell euclid is
1.13-1.15x and cc 1.31-1.39x the old form on one core, and at least as fast in
teams; distances change by at most 2 ulp. The benchmark's middle team is half
the threads.

        !> The euclid sums of one pair over angular bins 1..np, sum_k w_k (FT(CTF2) x FT(REF2) - 2 FT(X.CTF) x FT(REF)*),
    !! into acc. Plain array dummies: the compiler then knows unit stride and no overlap and vectorizes the bins.
    subroutine euclid_sums( np, n1, ldr, nk, ctf2, ref2, xctf, ref, wk, acc )
        integer,     intent(in)    :: np, n1, ldr, nk
        complex(sp), intent(in)    :: ctf2(n1,nk), ref2(n1,nk), xctf(n1,nk), ref(ldr,nk)
        real(dp),    intent(in)    :: wk(nk)
        complex(sp), intent(inout) :: acc(n1)
        complex(sp) :: c
        integer     :: kk, p
        acc = cmplx(0.,0.,kind=sp)
        do kk = 1,nk
            do p = 1,np
                c = ctf2(p,kk) * ref2(p,kk)
                c = c - 2.0 * xctf(p,kk) * conjg(ref(p,kk))
                acc(p) = cmplx(acc(p) + wk(kk) * c, kind=sp)
            enddo
        enddo
    end subroutine euclid_sums
