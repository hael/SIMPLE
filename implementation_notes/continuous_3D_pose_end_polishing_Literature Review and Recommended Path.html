    Literature Review and Recommended Path for Forward-Model Matching and Reference-Induced Pose Bias in Cryo-EM Polishing¶

    Executive conclusion¶

    The literature supports the conclusion that the current scientific production gate should remain closed, but it changes one important part of the previous interpretation.

    The REALSPACE_CLIP_NATIVE_FFT result establishes that the simulator and the current PCG/polishing model implement different discrete forward operators. Real-space cropping/windowing is not merely an amplitude operation: multiplication by a finite real-space window produces convolution in Fourier space and introduces correlations between Fourier coefficients. Cryo-EM literature explicitly discusses this effect for real-space masks and finite particle boxes. [1]

    Therefore, for data produced as

    [ y = F_N C F_P^{-1}G_P(R)V, ]

    the scientifically matched forward operator is

    [ A(R)=W G_P(R), \qquad W\equiv F_N C F_P^{-1}. ]

    The corresponding pose derivative is

    [ J_{\mathrm{matched}} = WJ_P, ]

    exactly as proposed.

    However, the literature leads to a stronger recommendation:

    The same matched operator must also be used when reconstructing the reference volume. [2–4]

    If PCG reconstructs the map with

    [ A_{\mathrm{old}}(R)=G_N(R) ]

    while the measurements were generated by (WG_P(R)), then the reconstruction itself can partially absorb the forward-model discrepancy. Polishing correct poses against that reconstructed map can subsequently convert a relatively small image-domain modeling error into a much larger orientation displacement.

    Consequently, the observed 4–6° drift should not yet be classified conclusively as an independent reference-bias phenomenon. It may be reference-induced, but the reference itself was apparently produced under the mismatched forward model.

    My recommended sequence is therefore:

    1. Match the forward model and Jacobian.
    2. Match the PCG reconstruction forward and adjoint operators.
    3. Reconstruct a new reference using the matched system.
    4. Repeat the exact-pose stationarity experiment against that new reconstruction.
    5. Only if substantial drift remains should the project move to explicit reference-bias countermeasures such as cross-fitted references, frequency/SNR weighting, pose priors, or eventually joint reconstruction/refinement.

    That ordering is the main conclusion of this literature review.

    1. What the REALSPACE_CLIP_NATIVE_FFT diagnostic established¶

    Your measurements are unusually clean:

    Test boundary Residual Interpretation
    PCG vs. padded projection (1.54\times10^{-4}) consistent
    Fourier round trip (1.28\times10^{-4}) consistent
    real-space clip/native FFT (1.38\times10^{-2}) inconsistent

    This is roughly a 90–108× increase relative to the two preceding control residuals.

    That pattern is exactly what would be expected when an additional spatial window is inserted into only one branch of the calculation.

    If

    [ p_P = F_P^{-1}G_P(R)V ]

    is the padded projection in real space and (C) extracts its central native-sized field of view, then

    [ C p_P ]

    is mathematically equivalent to multiplying the padded image by a rectangular spatial window and retaining that region.

    The convolution theorem then gives, schematically,

    [ \mathcal F{p_P c} =

    \widehat p_P * \widehat c. ]

    Cryo-EM analyses of masking explicitly note that real-space masking convolves the corresponding Fourier coefficients and can generate correlations between them. [1]

    Therefore,

    [ F_N C F_P^{-1}G_P(R)V ]

    is generally not equivalent to simply evaluating

    [ G_N(R)V ]

    on the native Fourier grid.

    They become approximately equivalent only under restrictive conditions—for example when the projection is sufficiently well confined inside the native real-space box that cropping effectively removes zero signal, while all Fourier coordinate and normalization conventions also agree.

    Your experiment demonstrates empirically that those conditions are not met.

    The frequency dependence is also consistent with a windowing discrepancy. Your low-frequency mismatch is only about 1%, while the middle band reaches 12.3% and the high-frequency correlation falls to 0.152. Window functions redistribute spectral information across frequencies rather than merely multiplying the complete spectrum by a constant. [1]

    The approximately quarter-scale transition is probably a separate FFT-normalization issue. If the padded dimensions are twice the native dimensions along both axes, a factor close to (1/4) is compatible with common 2-D FFT normalization conventions. The important point is that your global scale fit already removes most of that effect; it cannot remove the remaining frequency-dependent shape error.

    Thus the amplitude factor and the 1.38% residual should be treated as two different phenomena:

    [ \text{normalization mismatch} + \text{spectral-shape mismatch}. ]

    Only the first is removable by a scalar.

    2. The reconstruction operator now matters just as much as the polishing operator¶

    This is the most important consequence of the literature search.

    Modern cryo-EM reconstruction algorithms formulate projection and backprojection as paired linear operators. RELION, for example, performs Fourier-space projection by interpolation from an oversampled 3-D Fourier transform and performs the corresponding backprojection using the transpose of the interpolation matrix. [2]

    Likewise, iterative Fourier reconstruction formulations explicitly use

    [ A^\ast A, ]

    where (A) is the forward projector and (A^\ast) is its adjoint/backprojector. [3]

    The wider tomography literature has studied unmatched projector/backprojector pairs directly and shows that their convergence and reconstructed solutions can differ substantially from those obtained with matched operators. [4]

    For your simulator-matched model,

    [ A(R)=WG_P(R), ]

    with

    [ W=F_NCF_P^{-1}. ]

    Under unitary Fourier-transform notation, the adjoint is

    [ A^\ast(R) =

    G_P^\ast(R)W^\ast ]

    and

    [ W^\ast =

    F_P C^\ast F_N^{-1}, ]

    where (C^\ast) is the adjoint of cropping: zero-embedding the native image back into the padded real-space field.

    Exact prefactors depend on your FFT normalization convention, but the operator structure does not.

    This means a fully matched PCG system should conceptually use

    [ A^\ast A =

    G_P^\ast W^\ast W G_P, ]

    not

    [ G_N^\ast G_N. ]

    Why this changes the interpretation of the 4–6° drift¶

    Suppose the synthetic measurements satisfy

    [ y_i = A_{\mathrm{true}}(R_i)V_{\mathrm{true}} ]

    but the reference reconstruction solves approximately

    [ V_{\mathrm{rec}} =

    \arg\min_V \sum_i \left| y_i-G_N(R_i)V \right|^2. ]

    Then even with perfectly correct (R_i), the reconstruction is being asked to explain data generated by one system using another system.

    The reconstructed volume can compensate for part of that discrepancy:

    [ V_{\mathrm{rec}} \neq V_{\mathrm{true}}. ]

    Later, if pose polishing minimizes a residual against (V_{\mathrm{rec}}), changing (R_i) can improve agreement with distortions that were introduced into the map by the original reconstruction mismatch.

    That gives a plausible chain:

    [ \text{forward mismatch} \rightarrow \text{biased reconstructed reference} \rightarrow \text{pose drift}. ]

    This is an inference from your diagnostic plus established matched-operator theory; I did not find a paper demonstrating your exact 1.38%-to-4–6° scenario. But mathematically it is sufficiently plausible that it must be excluded experimentally before the drift is labeled an independent reference-bias problem. [2–4]

    3. The immediate implementation decision¶

    The explicit prototype you proposed remains the correct next step:

    [ \widehat y =

    F_NCF_P^{-1}G_P(R)V. ]

    Likewise,

    [ J_{\mathrm{matched}} =

    F_NCF_P^{-1}J_P. ]

    Because (W=F_NCF_P^{-1}) is linear and does not depend on pose,

    [ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \left[WG_P(R(\theta))V\right] =

    W \frac{\partial G_P(R(\theta))V}{\partial\theta}. ]

    Therefore no new analytical rotation derivative is required. Your already validated padded Jacobian can simply pass through the same (W).

    But the prototype should have two phases¶

    Phase A — pose-forward validation

    Use the explicit padded inverse FFT → real-space crop → native FFT operation for both prediction and Jacobian.

    Required results:

    [ r_{\mathrm{matched}} \approx r_{\mathrm{exactKB}} ]

    and the exact simulator pose should be stationary.

    This verifies the diagnosis at the particle level.

    Phase B — matched reconstruction

    Add the adjoint

    [ W^\ast ]

    to the reconstruction path and rebuild the PCG reference using

    [ A=WG_P,\qquad A^\ast=G_P^\ast W^\ast. ]

    Only after this reference exists should you perform the decisive reconstructed-reference pose test.

    4. A mandatory adjoint test should be added¶

    Before using the new operator inside PCG, I recommend making the adjoint identity a production gate:

    [ \langle Ax,y\rangle =

    \langle x,A^\ast y\rangle. ]

    Test it with random complex input volumes/images and multiple random orientations.

    Define

    [ \epsilon_{\mathrm{adj}} =

    \frac{ \left| \langle Ax,y\rangle- \langle x,A^\ast y\rangle \right| }{ \max( |\langle Ax,y\rangle|, |\langle x,A^\ast y\rangle|, \epsilon) }. ]

    For double precision, an error around (10^{-10})–(10^{-12}) should normally be achievable for an explicitly implemented linear operator, subject to the interpolation implementation. Single precision naturally requires a looser criterion.

    This test is more valuable than checking the reconstruction output alone because it tests the actual mathematical condition PCG expects.

    RELION's explicit use of the transpose projection interpolation for backprojection and iterative cryo-EM formulations based on (A^\ast A) make this a well-grounded design choice. [2,3]

    5. The decisive reconstructed-reference experiment¶

    After Phase A succeeds, I recommend the following experiment before implementing any reference-bias mitigation.

    Start from the known correct particle poses and the same synthetic observations.

    Construct:

    [ V_{\mathrm{old}} =

    \text{PCG reconstruction using }G_N ]

    and

    [ V_{\mathrm{matched}} =

    \text{PCG reconstruction using }WG_P \text{ and }G_P^\ast W^\ast. ]

    Then run matched pose polishing from the exact poses against both maps.

    The result has three scientifically distinct interpretations:

    Outcome Interpretation
    Drift disappears or becomes very small with (V_{\mathrm{matched}}) Most of the apparent reference bias was generated upstream by forward-model mismatch
    Drift decreases substantially but remains Mixed problem: operator mismatch plus genuine reference-induced pose bias
    Drift remains near 4–6° Reference/reconstruction bias is largely independent of the clipping mismatch

    This experiment is substantially more informative than immediately implementing leave-one-out references.

    6. What the cryo-EM literature says about genuine pose/reference bias¶

    If significant drift survives matched reconstruction, the literature strongly supports the existence of the second phenomenon you originally suspected.

    6.1 Reference-based orientation fitting can follow noise or map error¶

    Stewart and Grigorieff demonstrated that single-particle orientation refinement can overfit, particularly at spatial frequencies where signal is weak, and showed that resolution-dependent SNR weighting reduces the effect. [5]

    Shaikh and colleagues demonstrated reference dependence in projection matching and used cross-validation to expose it. Their core point is directly relevant: the data used to judge the quality of a fit should not be the same information that produced the reference used for fitting. [6]

    So, once operator mismatch is excluded,

    [ \arg\min_R |y-A(R)V_{\mathrm{rec}}|^2 ]

    does not have to equal

    [ R_{\mathrm{true}} ]

    when

    [ V_{\mathrm{rec}}\neq V_{\mathrm{true}}. ]

    That is not an optimizer defect. It is a property of the objective.

    6.2 High frequencies deserve less trust for orientation estimation¶

    Scheres and Chen's gold-standard work is particularly relevant. Their simulations showed that excluding unreliable high-frequency terms did not meaningfully degrade orientation determination, because low-to-medium frequencies contain most of the useful orientation information under typical cryo-EM SNR conditions. They therefore advocated independent refinement and/or frequency limitation to control overfitting. [7]

    This agrees remarkably well with your frequency diagnostic: the high-frequency portion is precisely where agreement is currently weakest.

    Even after the forward model is fixed, frequency-dependent weighting should therefore remain part of the methodological design.

    6.3 A good polishing algorithm should use a pose prior¶

    RELION implements local angular searches statistically as orientation priors and uses a truncated Gaussian around the previous optimal orientation. [2,8]

    Current CryoSPARC local refinement likewise provides a Gaussian pose/shift prior specifically to penalize movement far from the input pose and recommends such a prior when iterative recentering might permit particle drift. [9]

    This is directly applicable to your use case because this is polishing, not ab-initio orientation determination.

    If the starting poses already contain substantial information, treating every alternative nearby orientation as equally plausible throws that information away.

    A natural objective is therefore

    [ \Phi(\delta) =

    \sum_k w_k \left| y_k- a\,A_k(R_0\exp(\delta))V \right|^2 + \delta^\mathsf{T}\Lambda_R^{-1}\delta, ]

    where:

    • (R_0) is the incoming pose,
    • (\delta) is a local rotation vector,
    • (w_k) represents frequency/SNR reliability,
    • (a) is the nuisance amplitude scale if required,
    • (\Lambda_R) represents expected pose uncertainty.

    The LM system then becomes approximately

    [ \left( J^\ast W_fJ+ \Lambda_R^{-1} \right)\Delta =

    J^\ast W_fr- \Lambda_R^{-1}\delta. ]

    This is a natural extension of the LM solver you have already validated; it does not require replacing LM.

    Regularizing rotations away from the exact minimizer can actually improve orientation accuracy when the objective is noisy or ill-conditioned. A 2023 SIAM study of cryo-EM orientation regularization found cases where deliberately regularized rotation estimates were closer to ground truth than unrestricted global minimizers. [10]

    6.4 Recent work strengthens the Bayesian argument¶

    A 2026 Acta Crystallographica D paper by Xu, Balanov, Singer and Bendory compares conventional maximum-correlation orientation estimation with a Bayesian/MMSE treatment. Their simulations show improved orientation estimates under low SNR and reduced model bias when the Bayesian estimator is incorporated into refinement. [11]

    I would not replace your LM implementation with MMSE estimation immediately, because your current controls show that LM itself works correctly.

    But this recent result reinforces a broader conclusion:

    A single unregularized best-fitting orientation is not necessarily the statistically best orientation estimate.

    That principle is highly relevant to the 4–6° drift problem.

    7. Cross-validation: what I recommend and what I do not¶

    Gold-standard independent half-map refinement is now standard because common-reference refinement can propagate correlated noise and inflate apparent agreement. [7]

    However, gold-standard half maps alone do not guarantee that every local region or pose estimate is unbiased. SIDESPLITTER demonstrated that local overfitting may remain even with independently refined halves when local SNR is poor. [12]

    Independent-particle validation has also been proposed explicitly as an additional cross-validation mechanism for cryo-EM reconstruction. [13]

    Therefore I recommend cross-fitting as a diagnostic [6,7,13]¶

    I would not start with literal leave-one-particle-out reconstruction. It is computationally expensive and unnecessary for answering the first question.

    Instead perform a two-fold swapped-reference experiment:

    [ V_A=\text{reconstruct particles in A}, ]

    [ V_B=\text{reconstruct particles in B}. ]

    Then refine

    [ A\text{-particles against }V_B ]

    and

    [ B\text{-particles against }V_A. ]

    Every tested particle is therefore absent from the reference against which it is polished.

    If higher map quality is required, move to (K)-fold cross-fitting, for example (K=5) or (10):

    [ V_{-g} =

    \operatorname{Recon} \left( \text{all groups except }g \right), ]

    and particles in group (g) are refined against (V_{-g}).

    This gives most of the conceptual benefit of leave-one-out without requiring one reconstruction per particle.

    Interpretation¶

    If drift is large with the particle-containing reference but strongly reduced with the excluded reference, self-information/reference leakage is important.

    If essentially the same drift occurs against excluded references, the bias is more likely caused by systematic properties of the reconstructed map: discretization, angular sampling, anisotropic resolution, regularization, structural heterogeneity, or other model mismatch.

    That distinction is scientifically much more useful than calling both phenomena “reference bias.”

    8. Frequency-limited polishing should be the next methodological control [5,7]¶

    Once the matched reconstruction experiment is complete, I recommend a controlled cutoff sweep.

    For each cutoff (f_c), optimize

    [ \Phi_{f_c}(R) =

    \sum_{|\mathbf k|\le f_c} w_{\mathbf k} \left| y_{\mathbf k} -

    A_{\mathbf k}(R)V \right|^2. ]

    Record simultaneously:

    [ \Delta\theta(f_c), ]

    [ r_{\mathrm{fit}}(f_c), ]

    and, for synthetic experiments,

    [ e_R(f_c) =

    d_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}( R_{\mathrm{estimated}}, R_{\mathrm{true}} ). ]

    The most important curve is not simply residual versus cutoff. It is:

    [ \text{pose error versus residual improvement}. ]

    If including additional high frequencies lowers fitted residual while increasing ground-truth pose error, you have direct evidence that the optimizer is fitting reference error rather than pose information.

    Stewart and Grigorieff's SNR-weighted alignment work and the gold-standard studies provide strong precedent for frequency-dependent weighting rather than treating all Fourier samples equally. [5,7]

    Longer term, I would prefer continuous reliability weighting over a hard cutoff:

    [ w_k \propto \text{estimated signal reliability at }k. ]

    Half-map FSC/SNR information is a natural source for such weights.

    9. Why I would not implement joint reconstruction and pose optimization yet¶

    Joint reconstruction/orientation optimization is scientifically legitimate. Published work formulates cryo-EM reconstruction and angular refinement as a joint optimization problem, and recent work continues to investigate regularized joint formulations. [10,14,16]

    But it is not the right next fix here.

    Joint optimization introduces another feedback loop:

    [ R \rightarrow V \rightarrow R \rightarrow V. ]

    If the forward operator is not yet correct, joint optimization gives the volume additional freedom to absorb the mismatch.

    Likewise, if pose updates are biased, reconstruction can reinforce those pose errors in the next iteration.

    The literature on overfitting and independent half refinement is essentially a warning about this feedback mechanism. [5–7,12]

    Joint optimization should therefore come only after:

    [ A ]

    is validated,

    [ A^\ast ]

    is validated,

    and the fixed-reference pose estimator is characterized.

    10. Recommended experimental sequence¶

    I would now organize the work into five gates.

    Gate 1 — matched particle forward operator¶

    Implement

    [ A(R)=F_NCF_P^{-1}G_P(R) ]

    and

    [ J(R)=F_NCF_P^{-1}J_P(R). ]

    Pass conditions:

    • simulator residual collapses from (1.38\times10^{-2}) toward the exact-KB/control level;
    • exact simulator poses become stationary;
    • perturbed-pose recovery still succeeds;
    • finite-difference versus analytic Jacobian remains consistent after applying (W).

    If this fails, remain entirely in forward-model debugging.

    Gate 2 — matched reconstruction operator¶

    Implement

    [ A^\ast=G_P^\ast W^\ast. ]

    Add the numerical adjoint test

    [ \langle Ax,y\rangle \approx \langle x,A^\ast y\rangle. ]

    Then reconstruct at known exact poses.

    Compare:

    [ V_{\mathrm{old}}, \quad V_{\mathrm{matched}}, \quad V_{\mathrm{truth}}. ]

    Evaluate the map error by frequency as well as globally.

    Gate 3 — reconstructed-reference stationarity¶

    Using the newly reconstructed (V_{\mathrm{matched}}), initialize every particle at its exact simulator pose and run matched polishing.

    This is the experiment that should determine the project's next scientific branch.

    If the 4–6° drift essentially disappears, the previous diagnosis should be revised to:

    forward-model mismatch propagated through reconstruction and manifested as apparent reference-induced pose drift.

    If substantial drift remains:

    a genuine reconstruction/reference-induced pose-estimation problem remains after system-model matching.

    Only then proceed to Gate 4.

    Gate 4 — determine the source of remaining reference bias¶

    Run, in this order:

    1. frequency-cutoff/SNR-weight sweep;
    2. swapped-half-map cross-fitting;
    3. (K)-fold excluded-group references if necessary;
    4. pose-prior sweep.

    For every configuration record both residual improvement and ground-truth pose error.

    Because this is synthetic data with known truth, take advantage of that information aggressively. It provides a stronger validation signal than FSC or residual alone.

    Gate 5 — production pose-polishing design¶

    If residual reference-induced drift remains, I would make the production estimator approximately:

    [ \boxed{ \min_{\delta,a} \sum_k w_k \left| y_k- a\,WG_P(R_0\exp\delta)V \right|^2 + \delta^\mathsf T\Lambda_R^{-1}\delta } ]

    with:

    • the matched finite-FOV forward operator;
    • matched Jacobian;
    • frequency/SNR weights;
    • a local pose prior;
    • independent/cross-fitted reference construction wherever practical;
    • strict half-set independence for validation.

    This combines the main lessons of the mature cryo-EM literature without replacing your already validated LM machinery. [2,5–10,13]

    11. What I would not do¶

    I would not treat the quarter-scale normalization factor as the solution. Your global amplitude fit already shows that scalar normalization cannot explain the spectral-shape discrepancy.

    I would not loosen the LM tolerances or modify the rotation derivatives. The existing controls argue strongly against that path.

    I would not attempt to “correct” the reconstructed reference empirically while leaving the underlying system matrix inconsistent.

    I would not add a Hann/Tukey/soft window only to the projection model. A soft window can reduce edge-related Fourier artifacts, and soft masks are commonly preferred to hard masks in cryo-EM, but any deliberate window must be applied consistently to the measured particles and modeled projections. [1,15]

    I would not use joint reconstruction/refinement as a way to hide the fixed-reference drift.

    And I would not yet conclude that 4–6° is an unavoidable scientific limit of reconstructed-map polishing.

    12. Production implementation after the explicit prototype¶

    The explicit

    [ G_P \rightarrow F_P^{-1} \rightarrow C \rightarrow F_N ]

    implementation is the right diagnostic because it reproduces the simulator transparently.

    Performance should not influence this first version.

    If it passes, then optimize.

    Because the crop is fixed and separable, (W) has substantial exploitable structure. In Fourier space, central rectangular cropping corresponds to convolution/interpolation with the transform of the rectangular window, and the 2-D operation can be expressed through separable 1-D kernels. That suggests eventual implementations based on precomputed convolution/interpolation operators rather than two FFTs for every pose evaluation. The broader cryo-EM literature already uses gridding, oversampling, Kaiser–Bessel kernels and NUFFT-type methods precisely to obtain accurate Fourier interpolation efficiently. [2,3]

    But I would derive that optimized operator only after the explicit FFT–clip–FFT version becomes the numerical oracle.

    Every optimized implementation should be regression-tested against that oracle.

    13. Final scientific classification¶

    Based on your experiments plus the literature, I would now revise the classification slightly.

    Established¶

    The 1.38% discrepancy is a genuine forward-model mismatch caused by the real-space clipping/native-FFT path.

    It is not explained by LM, Jacobian, KB derivatives, or the Fourier round trip.

    The correct simulator-matched particle operator is

    [ WG_P. ]

    Very likely¶

    The PCG reconstruction should use the same forward physics and its adjoint:

    [ WG_P \quad\text{and}\quad G_P^\ast W^\ast. ]

    Otherwise the reconstructed reference can incorporate compensating distortions.

    Not yet established¶

    The 4–6° reconstructed-reference drift is not yet proven to be an independent methodological reference-bias problem.

    That was a reasonable earlier interpretation, but the newly localized forward mismatch creates an upstream mechanism that can contaminate the reference itself.

    If drift survives matched reconstruction¶

    Then the cryo-EM literature strongly supports treating it as a genuine reference/objective-induced pose-estimation problem.

    The preferred remedies would be, in order: [5–13]

    1. frequency/SNR-aware orientation fitting;
    2. cross-fitted or otherwise independent references for diagnosis and validation;
    3. Gaussian/local priors on polishing updates;
    4. probabilistic/Bayesian orientation estimation if stronger robustness is required;
    5. joint reconstruction and pose optimization only after the preceding pieces are validated.

    14. Final recommendation¶

    Do not branch into a large reference-bias redesign yet.

    First implement the explicit matched-window forward operator and Jacobian. Assuming that passes, immediately extend the same operator to PCG reconstruction with its true adjoint and rebuild the reference from scratch. [2–4]

    Then repeat the exact-pose polishing experiment.

    That single experiment will tell you whether the project has:

    [ \boxed{\text{one coupled operator-consistency problem}} ]

    or

    [ \boxed{\text{an operator problem plus a genuine pose/reference-bias problem}}. ]

    If the second case is confirmed, the literature gives a clear design direction: matched physics + independent references + frequency/SNR weighting + a statistically justified local pose prior, rather than further changes to LM itself. [2–10,13]

    Most relevant literature¶

    Forward/reconstruction consistency: RELION's reconstruction formulation explicitly pairs Fourier interpolation with its transpose during backprojection, while iterative reconstruction literature formulates the problem through (A^\ast A). Unmatched projector/backprojector literature provides the broader inverse-problem justification for taking this seriously. [2–4]

    Windowing: Cryo-EM analyses explicitly state that real-space masking produces Fourier-space convolution and correlations; finite particle boxes also impose implicit spatial boundaries. [1]

    Noise/reference bias: Stewart & Grigorieff showed orientation-refinement overfitting and the value of SNR-dependent weighting. Shaikh et al. demonstrated reference dependence and cross-validation in projection matching. [5,6]

    Independent refinement/frequency limitation: Scheres & Chen demonstrated the rationale for independent half-set refinement and showed that withholding unreliable high frequencies need not significantly damage orientation accuracy. [7]

    Regularization: RELION implements Gaussian-type orientation priors for local searches. Diepeveen et al. provide a modern mathematical treatment showing that regularization can produce rotations closer to ground truth than unrestricted optimization. [2,8,10]

    Independent validation: Ortiz et al. show the value of a completely independent particle control set for detecting refinement overfitting. [13]

    Current Bayesian direction: Xu et al. (2026) show that Bayesian/MMSE orientation estimators can outperform standard best-correlation estimates and reduce model bias under difficult SNR conditions. [11]
