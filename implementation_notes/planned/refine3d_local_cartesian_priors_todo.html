    Making Cartesian continuous refinement as good as the polar search, without losing its speed¶

    Updated 2026-11-11. Active work plan. Delivered implementation and historical evidence are in the done record.

    1. The objective¶

    refine3D_local can refine a particle's 3D pose two ways:

    • the polar route (refine=prob_neigh), which scores a grid of candidate poses and picks one from them;
    • the Cartesian route (refine=cont), which slides continuously downhill from the pose we already have.

    We have measured that the polar route produces better maps, and runs about five times slower.

    The goal of this project is to make the Cartesian route produce maps as good as the polar route, while staying substantially faster than it.

    The budget: the Cartesian route may become up to about twice as expensive as it is today. That still leaves it roughly 2.5 times faster than polar. Anything that buys quality inside that budget is on the table; anything that spends more needs an explicit decision.

    Everything else in this document serves that objective. If a task does not help close the quality gap or protect the speed advantage, it is not a priority.

    A note on what came before¶

    Most of the recent work on this code added an optional penalty called cont_prior, which discourages a particle from moving far during one refinement. That work is complete and tested, and section 7 lists the loose ends.

    But be clear about its status: the penalty is not the reason the polar route wins, and tuning it is unlikely to close the gap. It restrains motion; the evidence in section 3 says the Cartesian route's problem is that it cannot move to the right answer in the first place. Treat cont_prior as finished infrastructure that we may use later, not as the current line of attack.

    2. Vocabulary¶

    The rest of this document uses these terms. If they are already familiar, skip to section 3.

    Pose. The five numbers that place a particle image against the 3D reference: three for the rotation, two for the shift in the image plane.

    View direction and in-plane angle. A 3D rotation splits naturally into two parts: which direction the molecule is facing (the view direction, two numbers), and how much the resulting 2D projection is spun around in the image plane (the in-plane angle, one number). This distinction matters a great deal below.

    Loss. A number saying how badly a pose fits the image. Lower is better. Both routes use the same kind of loss, but they normalize it differently, which is why section 5 insists on checking they agree before comparing them.

    Posterior. The probability distribution over poses, given this image. A sharp posterior means the image pins the pose down; a broad one means many poses fit nearly as well. A multimodal posterior means there are several separated poses that each fit well — the image is genuinely ambiguous.

    Basin. A valley in the loss landscape. Downhill search from inside one basin can only ever reach the bottom of that basin, never a better one on the far side of a ridge.

    MAP versus draw. The MAP (maximum a posteriori) pose is the single best one. A draw is a random pick, where better poses are more likely but worse ones are still possible. Committing a draw rather than the MAP lets a particle escape a wrong answer on a later iteration. The polar route draws; the Cartesian route takes the best.

    Tempering. Flattening the posterior before drawing from it, so that worse poses get picked more often than they strictly deserve. Controlled by prob_beta (default 0.3; below 1 means flatter, i.e. more exploratory). It is a way of keeping options open early in a refinement.

    Calibration. Whether a stated uncertainty is honest: if we say we are 90% sure the pose is within some cone, is the truth inside that cone 90% of the time? This matters in section 8, not before.

    3. Why the polar route wins¶

    Three differences. They are listed in the order I believe matters, but section 4 exists precisely because that order is a hypothesis and we can test it cheaply before committing to any of it.

    Difference 1: the Cartesian route has a wall in the in-plane angle¶

    Both routes limit how far a particle may move from its stored pose, using the same parameter, athres_cont (10 degrees). But they apply it to different things.

    The polar route limits the view direction to 10 degrees, and then considers every in-plane angle, all 360 degrees of it (simple_eul_prob_tab_neigh.f90:764, and the in-plane scan in simple_polarft_corr.f90:697).

    The Cartesian route limits the whole rotation to 10 degrees (simple_cartft_pose_opt.f90:745). An in-plane rotation counts against that same budget. So if a particle needs to turn 8 degrees in view direction, it has only about 6 degrees of in-plane left — and it can never fix an in-plane error larger than 10 degrees at all. Trial poses outside the bound are rejected before they are even evaluated.

    This is an artificial wall, it is the largest difference in what the two routes are allowed to do, and removing it costs nothing. It does not by itself make the Cartesian search find a distant in-plane angle — downhill search still has to get there — but at present it is forbidden even from trying.

    A smaller related point: the polar bound folds over the molecule's symmetry and the Cartesian bound does not, so for symmetric molecules the Cartesian support is narrower than the number suggests.

    Difference 2: one downhill run versus a hundred and fifty candidates plus a lottery¶

    The Cartesian route performs exactly one downhill optimization per particle per iteration, starting from the stored pose, and keeps the result only if it is better (simple_strategy3D_cont.f90:193, simple_cartft_pose_opt.f90:423). It is deterministic and strictly monotone.

    Two consequences, both bad. It cannot cross a ridge, so a particle that starts in the wrong basin stays there — not just this iteration, but every iteration, forever. And when the image is genuinely ambiguous, it silently commits to one interpretation with no record that there were others.

    The polar route scores roughly 150 candidate directions inside the cone, each at its own in-plane angle, and then draws the committed pose from a tempered posterior over all of them (simple_eul_prob_tab_neigh.f90:961, simple_eul_prob_tab_utils.f90:1633). It can commit a pose that is worse than the current one, which is exactly how a particle climbs out of a false basin. The code even counts how often the draw differs from the best candidate, because it usually does.

    Difference 3: the polar route hedges in the reconstruction; the Cartesian route does not¶

    When a particle's pose is ambiguous, the polar route does not force a choice. It inserts the particle into the 3D reconstruction at up to three poses at once, weighted by posterior probability (topk, default 3; simple_eul_prob_tab_utils.f90:1813 and simple_matcher_3Drec.f90:314). Ambiguous particles therefore contribute a blurred, honest average instead of a confident wrong answer.

    The Cartesian route has no equivalent of this at all. Every particle enters the reconstruction at exactly one pose.

    This is a strong suspect for the quality gap, because its effect is on the map directly rather than on the poses, and because a pose-error metric would not reveal it.

    What this adds up to¶

    The Cartesian route is a good optimizer and a poor estimator. It finds the bottom of its valley efficiently and then behaves as though that valley were certainly the right one. The polar route is a worse optimizer — its answers sit on a grid — but it represents the uncertainty, and that is what is reaching the map.

    The encouraging part: of the three differences, one is free to remove, one is nearly free, and the expensive one (scoring many candidates) is where our evaluation budget actually is. See section 6.

    4. Step 0: find out which difference actually matters — four runs, no code¶

    Before writing any code, attribute the gap. All three differences above are active in the polar route simultaneously, and we can switch two of them off from the command line:

    • committing the best candidate instead of a draw;
    • topk=1, which inserts each particle at one pose instead of three (documented as reproducing the single-pose path exactly).

    Run the polar route four times on the same project, same seeds, same references:

    Run Commit rule Reconstruction
    1 draw topk=3 (this is the current polar route)
    2 draw topk=1
    3 best topk=3
    4 best topk=1

    Run 4 is the polar route stripped of both mechanisms: a wide discrete search that commits its best candidate at one pose. If run 4 is still much better than the Cartesian route, the gap is mostly the search support and we should attack difference 1. If run 4 falls back toward Cartesian quality, the gap is the estimator and we should attack differences 2 and 3 — and the table tells us which of the two is carrying it.

    This costs four polar runs and no development time, and it decides the order of everything that follows. Do this first.

    Record FSC, cFAR and, on synthetic data, pose error against truth. Keep the particle identities so the comparison can be paired (section 7).

    5. The one check that must pass before cross-route comparison means anything¶

    The two routes compute their loss with different normalizations: the Cartesian route scales by whitened particle power over four, the polar route by a different expression involving the polar ring size. Until we have confirmed that both report the same number for the same physical pose on the same particle, any statement of the form "polar scored this better" is confounded with a units bug.

    So: freeze references, noise, mask, CTF, crop, band and pose; take poses found by the polar route; rescore them with the Cartesian calculator; compare. Check the special low-frequency bins, the interpolation error and the normalization separately. Set the agreement tolerance from interpolation accuracy, not from the observed difference.

    This is a one-off diagnostic, not a feature. It is cheap, it protects every comparison downstream, and it is likely to surface real bugs. Do it alongside step 0.

    6. The work, in order¶

    Costs are in model evaluations per particle. The Cartesian route currently averages 4.7 per particle (measured, beta-galactosidase). The 2x budget therefore allows about 5 extra evaluations. For scale, the polar route uses roughly 300 to 450 evaluations per particle, but each is individually much cheaper, which is how a seventy-fold difference in evaluation count becomes only a five-fold difference in wall-clock time.

    That per-evaluation ratio is inferred, not measured. Measure it early: one benchmarked run of each route on the same project gives nanoseconds per polar row from the fill profile and nanoseconds per Cartesian pass from the existing per-pixel timing. Every cost estimate below rests on it.

    6.1 Remove the in-plane wall — cost: zero¶

    Split the single rotation bound into a view-direction bound and a separate in-plane bound, so the two no longer compete for one budget. Mechanically: decompose the trial rotation relative to the seed into view change and in-plane change and test them separately, instead of the single combined test at simple_cartft_pose_opt.f90:745. Fold the view bound over the point group, as the polar route does.

    Done when: a particle whose in-plane angle is wrong by 30 degrees can be corrected, with the view-direction bound still enforced at 10 degrees; prior-off results are otherwise unchanged.

    6.2 Let the commit be a draw rather than strictly the best — cost: zero¶

    Replace "keep the result only if it beats the seed" with a draw over {seed, result} at the particle's temperature, times prob_beta. The machinery exists and is representation-agnostic: draw_posterior_row and posterior_temperature take a plain array of losses and a temperature. Supply the Cartesian temperature, which is the same quantity the Cartesian loss is already normalized by.

    This is the cheapest available escape mechanism from a false basin, and it costs no extra evaluations.

    Done when: a fixture with a known false basin escapes it over several iterations with the draw enabled and never escapes with it disabled; the result is reproducible under the keyed random stream across thread counts and partitions.

    6.3 Weight the reconstruction over several poses — cost: zero to small¶

    Give the Cartesian route the topk behaviour. The insertion machinery (prob_insert_pose, prob_insert_table, prob_insert_poses, prob_insert_cone) is geometric and has no polar dependency; the consumer in simple_matcher_3Drec.f90:314 already handles a particle with no extras as the plain single-pose path.

    The question this raises is which several poses, since the Cartesian route currently produces one. Either this follows 6.4, or an interim version inserts the single converged pose with a weight reflecting how well determined it is. Decide after step 0 says how much topk is worth.

    6.4 Several starting points, then weight the results — cost: about 4.7 per extra start¶

    Run the downhill search from two or three starting points instead of one and keep all converged results, then draw the commit from them and insert the best few into the reconstruction with posterior weights. refine_pose already takes the pose as an in/out argument, so a multi-start loop needs no new optimizer, only a per-start record and a selection rule.

    Two starts fits the budget. Where the extra starts come from matters more than how many there are: candidates from a coarse scan (6.5) are worth more than random perturbations.

    6.5 A cheap coarse scan to choose the starting points — cost: to be measured¶

    The principled version of 6.4: before optimizing, evaluate the loss on a coarse grid — in-plane angles at the seed direction are the cheap and probably highest value choice — and start the optimizer from the best two or three distinct basins. This is the Cartesian analogue of what the polar route does, at a tiny fraction of the candidate count.

    Prerequisite: the calculator has no loss-only entry point. Every evaluation currently computes the gradient and all fifteen Hessian entries, which a scan does not need. Add a loss-only path first; the saving is substantial and it is useful independently.

    Budget honestly before building: 36 in-plane angles at 10-degree spacing, even at a third of the cost of a full evaluation, is roughly 12 full-evaluation equivalents — over budget on its own. A coarser scan, or scanning only particles flagged as uncertain, is the way to fit. Measure the loss-only cost first, then choose the grid.

    6.6 Only then, the fuller posterior treatment¶

    Sections 8 and 9 describe credible regions, posterior-derived search bounds and fitted population widths. These are worth doing but they are not the shortest path to the objective, and they depend on calibration work that depends in turn on the section 5 gate. Do not start them before 6.1 to 6.4 have been measured.

    7. How to avoid fooling ourselves¶

    Three recurring traps, all of which have already cost us time.

    Measure whether a change does anything before measuring whether it helps. The cont_prior penalty was compared across a sixteen-fold range of widths and every arm returned the same FSC to three digits. That is the signature of a term that is being added to something much larger. Before any comparison campaign, confirm the mechanism under test can change the answer at the settings used: report the penalty actually applied, the width actually used and the particle power alongside the change in data loss, and include one deliberately extreme setting as a positive control. A build that compiles and tests that pass prove neither.

    Do not let a change be scored on the quantity it suppresses. Our convergence rule declares a particle stable when it stops moving, and several of the mechanisms here change how far particles move. A change that makes everything stop early will look like fast convergence and may be a worse map. Always compare at a matched iteration budget as well as at natural stopping, and report the iteration count at convergence alongside the quality.

    Use the pairing. Our arms share particles, seeds, references and starting poses. Comparing one FSC number per arm throws away thousands of paired observations and gives no uncertainty at all — which is why five arms differing by a factor of sixteen all reported 3.33 Å and we could not tell whether that meant "no effect" or "no measurement". Compare per-particle differences between arms with a paired bootstrap or signed-rank analysis. That removes between-particle variance and produces a confidence interval, which is what turns "no difference" into "no difference larger than X" — the statement needed to close a question. Report shell-wise FSC differences, not only the crossing, and on synthetic data report the upper quantiles of pose error, since the tails are where the failures live.

    Fix calibration and equivalence margins before the runs that judge them, not after.

    8. Correctness items to fix along the way¶

    These came out of a code review on 2026-11-11. None blocks section 6; fold each into whatever change next touches that code.

    The rotation penalty's curvature is documented wrongly. For a proper rotation, the Gauss-Newton block of the Frobenius-residual rotation penalty is exactly 2*w*I everywhere, not only near the seed. The comparison table in the done record rejects an alternative for having constant curvature, which is the property the chosen form also has. Derive and test the penalty, its gradient, its exact Hessian and the Gauss-Newton block separately, then correct the description. Before replacing the computed loop with the constant, check the real input contract: stored matrices come from single-precision Euler angles, so they are not exactly orthogonal, and the identity holds exactly only for proper rotations.

    The identifiability check does not check identifiability. A successful Cholesky factorization with a relative pivot floor establishes that the system is numerically solvable, not that the data determine the pose. A badly conditioned or uniformly weak system passes, and the prior then quietly rescues a direction the data do not constrain, while the code reports a data-identifiable result. Decide whether to narrow the claim to "numerically solvable" or add a genuine weak-data rejection. Note that a condition number alone will not catch a well-conditioned but uniformly uninformative system; both failures need covering.

    The stationarity test is an absolute threshold on a scale-dependent quantity. Convergence is declared at a gradient norm below 1e-8, but that gradient's scale depends on band, particle power and the rotation scaling. Build fixtures that change the scaling while preserving the physical problem, and define a relative test. This matters more once the search domain widens.

    Self-conjugate frequency bins. The documentation discusses the zero-frequency bin alone. The general case is bins where k = -k (mod N) in both coordinates; enumerate them, confirm how they are stored, counted and included in the band, and state the convention. Entire Nyquist rows are not individually self-conjugate — only the fixed points are. This is a documentation correction unless the audit finds the band actually includes one.

    Minor. prepare_pose_data_system performs a factorization whose result is discarded, using it only as a guard flag before the real combined solve; it reads like a bug and needs a comment. Dummy argument alignment regressed in the two prior setters, and three renamed declarations carry stray spacing (simple_parameters::l_cont_followup, simple_convergence::calc_cont_pose_stats, simple_commanders_refine3D::exec_refine3D_workflow).

    9. Later: honest uncertainty, and using it¶

    Once the Cartesian route produces several candidate poses with weights (section 6.4), it becomes possible to ask what its uncertainty actually is and to use that to make the search cheaper. That is the long-term prize: a search that spends effort where the pose is uncertain and skips particles that are already pinned down. The design below is carried forward and is not implemented.

    One explicit density. Work in physical negative log likelihood, E(R,s) = T*L(R,s) + U(R,s), with T the conversion from normalized loss and U the penalty. The two routes must agree on E at identical poses — this is the section 5 gate, and it blocks everything here.

    Credible regions, not fixed multiples of a width. For a candidate set with weights q, the smallest cone retaining 1-epsilon of the mass is the honest bound. Three cautions. The weights must account for the volume each candidate represents, so non-uniform grids cannot be counted equally. Several separate 99% bounds on rotation, in-plane angle and shift do not give 99% joint coverage; budget the lost mass jointly. And a quantile of a posterior that has already been cropped to a search domain only describes mass inside that crop — it cannot justify cropping further, so start broad and check the boundary.

    Beware tails estimated from few candidates. A 99% bound is set by the least likely candidates, exactly where a small weighted sample is least reliable. Gate it on how many candidates effectively carry the weight, and fall back to a broad search when that number is too small; otherwise the tightest bounds get assigned to the worst-estimated particles, which loses them irrecoverably. There is an existing entropy-based readout (prob_neff_readout) to build on, though it measures concentration rather than sampling adequacy and the distinction needs care.

    Uncertainty from curvature, if the posterior is a single narrow peak. The local information matrix gives an approximate covariance. Two requirements: build it from likelihood and prior only, never from the optimizer's damping or its empirical curvature scaling; and marginalize properly — rotation uncertainty with shifts integrated out is the Schur complement (H_RR - H_Rs H_ss^-1 H_sR)^-1, not H_RR^-1. Radii scale with the square root of a chi-square quantile whose degrees of freedom differ by coordinate: two for a direction cone, three for a full rotation, two for shifts. A fixed multiple of the width is not the same retained mass in those different spaces.

    Fitted population widths, carefully. Widths can be fitted across particles rather than set by hand, using completed-pass statistics within each half map. Two hazards. Reusing a particle's own posterior as its next prior and then multiplying by the same image again counts the same evidence twice; refinement iterations are passes of one optimization, not independent measurements. And the fit has a degenerate direction: a tight width shrinks the posterior moments, which shrinks the next width. Record the fitted width per pass per half and stop on repeated decrease without held-out improvement.

    Symmetry. When distances are minimized over the point group, the reference distribution is not the unrestricted one. State which space every calibration claim is measured in and validate against enumeration for both C1 and a non-trivial group.

    Calibration testing. Coverage at two or three stated levels is a blunt instrument. On synthetic data, record the rank of the true pose within the posterior sample: uniform ranks mean honest uncertainty, a U shape means too narrow, a dome means too wide, a tilt means bias. Verify the expected rank distribution on an exact toy model before trusting it on real output.

    See also the polar Laplace-width proposal, which separates a search domain from a probabilistic width on the polar side. Its shsig is an RMS radius in native pixels; the Cartesian cont_prior_shift_sigma is a per-axis standard deviation in Angstroms. Convert before comparing.

    10. Current behaviour worth knowing before you touch the code¶

    • cont_prior=none|shift|pose applies to single-state Euclidean joint Cartesian refinement; none is the default. The penalty is re-centred on the stored pose at the start of each optimizer transaction, so it restrains the size of one update and does not accumulate across passes. It is a step restraint, not a belief about where the pose is.
    • A positive cont_prior_shift_sigma is a per-axis width in Angstroms, converted internally to native pixels by smpd. Zero selects an estimator built from all active particles' stored shift increments, with a floor. cont_prior_rot_sigma is a per-axis small-angle width in degrees.
    • That estimator is driven by the increments the penalty suppresses, so it can tighten itself. The floor is what stops it. Treat the floor as load-bearing.
    • Acceptance and optimizer stopping use the penalized objective; published scores and noise residuals remain data-only.
    • The Cartesian solve returns a local penalized optimum, not a posterior.
    • Cartesian bounds (full rotation, shift disk) and polar bounds (view direction, shift box per axis) must be matched explicitly before any comparison.
